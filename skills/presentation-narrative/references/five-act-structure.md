# Five-Act Structure for Technical Presentations

Most technical presentations fail because they're structured as information dumps: "Here's what I know about X." The audience sits through 20 minutes of facts and leaves with nothing actionable. 

The five-act structure creates a narrative where the audience *needs* to hear each slide because the previous one opened a question.

## Act 1: Provocation (1-2 slides)

**Purpose:** Break the audience's default mental model. Make them realize something they assumed is wrong, incomplete, or more nuanced than they thought.

**What makes a good provocation:**
- It targets a *specific* belief the audience holds (not a vague topic)
- It creates genuine cognitive dissonance — "wait, really?"
- It's emotionally engaging, not just intellectually interesting
- It doesn't give away the resolution — it opens a question

**Patterns:**
- The misread: "You've been reading this sentence wrong for 20 years"
- The reversal: "The people who invented X now say it's broken"
- The hidden cost: "This thing you do every day is costing you more than you think"
- The false choice: "Everyone argues A vs B, but the real question is C"

**Common failure:** Starting with background or definitions. "Today I want to talk about Agile. Agile is..." kills energy. Start with the disruption, provide context after.

**Slide density:** Very sparse. One quote, one question, or one statistic that lands hard. No bullet points. No diagrams. Just impact.

## Act 2: Context (2-3 slides)

**Purpose:** Give the audience just enough history/background to understand why the provocation matters. This is NOT a comprehensive history lesson — it's the minimum viable context.

**What makes good context:**
- It answers "how did we get here?" in the fewest possible slides
- It establishes credibility — primary sources, named people, specific dates
- It makes the audience feel smarter, not lectured at
- It builds toward the provocation's resolution, not away from it

**Patterns:**
- The origin story: compressed history that leads to a key moment
- The setup: "Here's what was happening that made this inevitable"
- The cast of characters: introducing the key players with brief credentials

**Common failure:** Spending too long on context. Two slides of history feels informative; four slides of history feels like a lecture. Cut ruthlessly. If a historical fact doesn't serve the argument, cut it.

**Slide density:** Moderate. Can include a timeline diagram or a key data point. But don't overwhelm — the audience needs to feel grounded, not buried.

## Act 3: Evidence (2-3 slides)

**Purpose:** Build the case with specific, verifiable proof. After the provocation opened a question and context framed it, evidence answers it.

**What makes good evidence:**
- It's specific: names, dates, quotes, data — not vague claims
- It's varied: mix data slides with quote slides with diagram slides
- It accumulates: each piece of evidence builds on the last
- It's attributed: "Martin Fowler wrote..." not "some people think..."

**Patterns:**
- The data point: a single compelling statistic displayed prominently
- The primary source: a direct quote from an authority the audience respects
- The pattern: showing that multiple independent sources converge on the same conclusion
- The emotional testimony: the founders' own disillusionment, a team's real experience

**Common failure:** Presenting evidence as a flat list. "Point 1... Point 2... Point 3..." doesn't build momentum. Instead, create a crescendo — start with the most surprising piece, build through corroboration, and peak with the most emotionally resonant.

**Slide density:** Varies by type. Data slides are sparse (big number + label). Quote slides are sparse (quote + attribution). Diagram slides need the figure but minimal surrounding text.

## Act 4: Reframe (1-2 slides)

**Purpose:** Give the audience a new mental model to replace the one the provocation disrupted. This is the "so what?" — the synthesis that makes all the evidence actionable.

**What makes a good reframe:**
- It's simple enough to state in one sentence
- It feels like an insight, not an instruction
- It resolves the tension created by the provocation
- It makes the call to action feel obvious and natural

**Patterns:**
- The distinction: "The difference isn't X vs Y — it's *how* you do it"
- The spectrum: "It's not binary. Here's where the healthy middle is"
- The inversion: "What you thought was anti-X is actually the most X thing you can do"
- The framework: a simple model (2x2, spectrum, flowchart) that organizes the evidence

**Common failure:** Skipping the reframe entirely and jumping from evidence to "so here's what we should do." Without the reframe, the call to action feels arbitrary. The reframe is the bridge.

**Slide density:** Very sparse. A single diagram, a single statement, or a short framework. This needs to land — don't clutter it.

## Act 5: Action (1-2 slides)

**Purpose:** Give the audience something specific, concrete, and achievable to do. The call to action must feel like the natural, inevitable conclusion of everything that came before.

**What makes a good CTA:**
- It's specific: "Review this MR by Friday" not "think about documentation"
- It's achievable: something they can do this week, not this quarter
- It's earned: the narrative built the case; the CTA is the obvious next step
- It's respectful: it asks for engagement, not compliance

**Patterns:**
- The review: "We've drafted X. Your input shapes it. Here's the link."
- The experiment: "Try this for one sprint. See what happens."
- The conversation: "Let's discuss this. Here are the open questions."
- The commitment: "Who's willing to try this on their next project?"

**Common failure:** A CTA that doesn't follow from the narrative. If you could deliver the CTA without the preceding 12 slides, the talk failed as a narrative — it was just a preamble to an announcement.

**Slide density:** Minimal. The CTA itself, a link or next step, and nothing else. End clean.

## Slide-Count Budget

For a 15-minute talk at 90 seconds per slide:

| Component | Slides | Time |
|-----------|--------|------|
| Title | 1 | 30s (intro, settle) |
| Act 1: Provocation | 1-2 | 1.5-3 min |
| Act 2: Context | 2-3 | 3-4.5 min |
| Act 3: Evidence | 2-3 | 3-4.5 min |
| Act 4: Reframe | 1-2 | 1.5-3 min |
| Act 5: Action | 1-2 | 1.5-3 min |
| **Total** | **8-13** | **~15 min** |

If your outline exceeds 13 slides, you have too much material. Cut from Acts 2 and 3 first — they're the most likely to bloat.
